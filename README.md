# ExtremeRainfall
## Do informative priors improve extreme rainfall estimation? Evidence from a national-scale EVT comparison

Accurate estimation of extreme rainfall return levels is critical for hydrological design, yet is often limited by short data records and substantial uncertainty in the distribution tail. This study evaluates how the choice of inferential framework and prior specification influences estimates of extreme daily rainfall across 151 Australian stations. Annual maxima were modelled using the generalized extreme value (GEV) distribution, with parameters estimated using maximum likelihood, L-moments, Bayesian inference with weak priors, and Bayesian inference with informative quantile-based priors. Results show that Bayesian estimation with weak priors systematically increases uncertainty relative to maximum likelihood, with interval widths typically 30-40% larger across all stations. In contrast, the use of informative priors reduces uncertainty by approximately 20-40%, with the largest reductions observed at sites with shorter records or higher variability. Differences in return level estimates across methods are generally small compared to associated uncertainty. The inclusion of a large-scale climate covariate had minimal impact on annual maximum estimates, even at sites characterized by high rainfall extremes. These findings demonstrate that the effectiveness of Bayesian approaches depends on prior informativeness rather than the inferential framework itself, with quantile-based priors providing a practical means of stabilizing extreme rainfall estimates.

PRACTICAL APPLICATIONS:
This study clarifies when Bayesian approaches provide practical value for estimating extreme rainfall used in design and risk assessments. For most engineering applications, return levels for rare events are estimated from records that are shorter than the events being designed for, leading to substantial uncertainty in the upper tail. The results show that Bayesian methods do not automatically resolve this problem. When weak or default priors are applied, uncertainty increases relative to conventional maximum likelihood estimates. In contrast, when prior information is specified in terms of realistic rainfall magnitudes, uncertainty in 100-year return levels is typically reduced by 20-40%, particularly at sites with limited or highly variable records. This provides a practical pathway to stabilize design values without additional data, which is directly relevant for drainage design, flood estimation, and infrastructure planning. The analysis also indicates that including large-scale climate indicators does not materially change annual maximum estimates. The key implication is that improved prior specification, not increased model complexity, is the most effective way to obtain more reliable extreme rainfall estimates for engineering use.
